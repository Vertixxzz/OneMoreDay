# Independent Game Development and the Evolution of the Video Game Industry: Literature Review

*Working draft for the diploma project “Independent Game Development as a Catalyst for the Evolution of the Video Game Industry”*

## 1. Scope and approach

This review examines scholarship on independent game development as a possible contributor to change in the video game industry. It brings together work on the meaning of independence, historical industry change, development tools and distribution platforms, creative practice, and the economic and social conditions of independent production. The aim is not to demonstrate that independent games are inherently innovative. Instead, the review asks under what conditions independent developers may experiment with production methods, aesthetics, distribution, or game design, and what limits the broader influence of such experiments.

The review is a focused narrative review of 15 publications identified for the project, including conceptual articles, historical research, qualitative studies, and a case study of a single game’s development. These works were selected because they address different parts of the relationship between independent development and industry change. This is not a systematic review: the current source set does not result from a documented database search, predefined inclusion criteria, or a reproducible screening procedure. Therefore, it should be treated as a structured foundation for the diploma rather than an exhaustive map of the field. The methods and source set should be expanded if the final thesis requires a systematic review. Several empirical studies in the current set are geographically or platform-specific, and their findings should not be generalized beyond those settings without additional evidence. The selection spans 2015–2024 because the research is historical and conceptual: earlier scholarship is retained where it defines key terms or documents industry change, while newer studies contribute evidence on more recent production practices. A strict five-year cutoff would exclude relevant historical and foundational work. The date of publication alone is not treated as a measure of relevance; claims about current markets or platform conditions will require suitably current sources.

## 2. Defining “independent” and “indie”

A first difficulty is definitional. Garda and Grabarczyk (2016) argue that “indie” is not simply a synonym for “independent.” They distinguish three dimensions of independence: financial relations between developers and investors, creative relations between developers and intended audiences, and publishing relations between developers and publishers. Their framework is useful because these dimensions can vary independently: a studio may retain creative control while relying on a publisher, or self-publish while depending on external finance. This prevents the research from treating company size, visual style, or commercial status as sufficient proof of independence.

Pérez Latorre (2016) similarly questions a strict opposition between indie and mainstream games. He describes indie culture and production as involving hybridizations and ambiguities between alternative and mainstream practices. Read together, the two articles suggest that “independent” is better treated as a set of relationships and conditions than as a fixed genre. A game can be alternative in its design while participating in mainstream distribution and marketing systems.

This has a practical methodological consequence: the diploma should avoid selecting case studies solely because they are commonly labelled “indie.” The analysis should state why each title is included and what evidence is available concerning its development, publishing, or creative context. Where such information is unknown, independence should be described as a public classification or working assumption rather than a fully demonstrated fact.

## 3. Historical change and the indie–mainstream relationship

Tsang’s (2021) history of the British video game industry provides a long-term perspective on the relationship between grassroots production and industrial change. The study traces an industry that began with hobbyists and amateurs, passed through periods of consolidation and acquisition, and later saw new opportunities emerge through mobile games and digital distribution. This historical account challenges a simple story in which independent development is a recent disruption to an otherwise stable mainstream. Informal and small-scale creation was part of the industry’s earlier development as well.

The value of Tsang’s work lies in connecting production practices to wider structural conditions. Changes in tools, markets, and distribution can alter who is able to enter the field, but those opportunities are historically specific. Since the article focuses on Britain, its findings should not be generalized automatically to every national game industry. The diploma can use it as a documented historical case while being explicit about the geographical scope of the evidence.

Pérez Latorre’s (2016) account adds a cultural dimension: independent games do not necessarily remain outside the mainstream once their styles, audiences, or business models become commercially visible. The two sources therefore complement one another. Tsang helps explain how industry structures change over time, while Pérez Latorre cautions that “alternative” and “mainstream” are overlapping positions rather than stable opposites. Taken together, they support a more careful research question: not whether indie games stand apart from the industry, but how particular independent practices move through, challenge, or become incorporated into wider industry systems.

## 4. Development tools, production practices, and distribution

Keogh (2019) broadens the definition of game development beyond highly formalized studio production. His account includes hobbyists, amateurs, students, artists, and other creators whose processes may be informal or hybrid. This matters for research on independent development because participation is shaped not only by company structures but also by the availability of tools, communities, skills, and routes to publication. At the same time, a broader definition makes comparison harder: hobby projects, student work, and commercial indie studios do not face identical goals or constraints.

Chia et al. (2020) examine platformisation through game-making tools, especially Unity and Twine. Rather than treating platforms only as distribution services, they show that tools used to create games also shape production practices and communities. Their analysis complicates both optimistic claims that accessible tools simply democratize development and pessimistic accounts in which platform power determines all creative activity. Tools can enable participation while also structuring the conditions under which creators work. This is relevant to the practical project, but it does not by itself prove that a particular engine causes innovation; it provides a lens for understanding how technical affordances and constraints mediate creative choices.

Digital distribution is another major theme. Werning (2019a) analyzes Steam’s evolution from a distribution and update service into a platform with social and community features, showing how platform features can influence relationships among developers, players, publishers, and platform operators. In a separate study, Werning (2019b) examines itch.io and a corpus of almost 300 low-priced games to explore how a platform’s features can help shape a recognizable product category and forms of expression. These studies approach distribution from different directions: Steam illustrates the expansion of a major platform’s functions, while itch.io illustrates how the affordances of a smaller independent publishing platform can support particular kinds of games.

However, access to distribution should not be confused with discoverability or economic sustainability. Platform access can lower some barriers to release while creating dependence on store policies, recommendation systems, audiences, and competition. The platform studies therefore pair productively with research on labor and market pressures. They show that digital infrastructure changes what developers can do, but do not guarantee that players will find a game or that its creators will earn a sustainable income.

## 5. Creative processes and game-specific innovation

Kultima, Ojanen, and Nylund (2024) investigate the development of Noita over more than a decade using devlogs, prototypes, builds, interviews, and player engagement materials. Their timeline method organizes more than 150 events into seven phases, offering a way to examine how a game idea changes over a long development process. For the present diploma, the most useful contribution is methodological: innovation can be studied as a sequence of decisions, prototypes, influences, and revisions rather than inferred only from a finished product.

The Noita study is a single-game case study, so it cannot establish how typical that development process is across the independent sector. Nevertheless, it demonstrates why development histories are valuable evidence. If a diploma argues that a game introduced or popularized an approach, it should distinguish the design feature itself from the process by which it emerged, and should seek evidence of reception or influence rather than relying only on the game’s originality.

Phillips (2015) adds a community-level view of creative influence through informal norms around cloning and intellectual property. Drawing on interviews and workshop discussions, the article describes how independent developers may respond to perceived copying through public criticism and informal self-regulation. This complicates the idea that innovation is purely an individual act: the meaning of originality is negotiated in communities, and influence can be understood as both creative exchange and a source of conflict. The article also cautions against assuming that informal norms provide clear or consistent protection for creators.

## 6. Economic conditions, labor, and sustainability

A recurring limit on creative autonomy is the economic organization of independent work. Lipkin (2019) examines the “Indiepocalypse” discourse about increasing competition and weak commercial prospects in the PC game market, comparing that discourse with interviews conducted among 21 members of the New York City independent development community. His analysis emphasizes the interaction between market conditions and developers’ motivations to continue making games even when financial rewards are uncertain. The article is important because it treats precarious outcomes as more than a simple result of individual talent or effort: market structures and non-financial motivations can reinforce one another.

Pereira and Bernardes (2018) examine organizational and managerial practices through an exploratory study involving lead developers from seven Brazilian independent game companies. This empirical focus complements broad historical and conceptual work by examining how independent production is organized in practice. However, the relatively small, geographically situated sample means that findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than as universal rules for all independent studios.

Whitson, Simon, and Parker (2021) draw on more than 60 interviews and 120 surveys to examine entrepreneurship, relational labor, and sustainability in indie cultural production. Their work highlights less visible labor involved in sustaining creative production, including relationships and entrepreneurial work that may not be represented in the game itself. Young (2024), using ethnographic research in Toronto, examines everyday game-makers and the appeal of creative autonomy alongside precarious working conditions. Together, these studies complicate the common narrative that independent work is simply more free than studio employment: autonomy can be meaningful, but it may coexist with insecurity, long hours, and the need to manage work and professional identity.

Parker and Perks (2021) offer a focused example of these trade-offs in their study of livestreaming and small commercial indie developers in Toronto and Montréal. Livestreaming may support promotion, feedback, and community-building, but it can also require time and affect production practices; its benefits are not equally clear for every developer. This supports a broader conclusion that tools for visibility should not be treated as reliable routes to success. Their practical value depends on context, resources, audience, and the work required to maintain them.

Tomkinson and Harper (2022) situate game production within broader debates about corporate control, platformisation, and resistance. Their analysis suggests that digital tools and player communities can challenge some forms of control, while the high costs of production and platform power may also reinforce concentration. Read with Lipkin, Whitson et al., Young, and Parker and Perks, this produces a balanced picture: independent development may create room for alternative work and expression, but this space is structured by economic and platform conditions that can constrain its sustainability.

## 7. Synthesis and research gap

Across these publications, four connected conclusions emerge. First, independence is multidimensional and historically situated rather than a label that can be inferred from a game’s appearance. Second, accessible tools and digital distribution can broaden participation and support novel production practices, but they also create new dependencies on platforms and markets. Third, creative experimentation is embedded in social and organizational processes: it emerges through tools, communities, prototypes, labor, and cultural negotiations. Fourth, creative autonomy and economic sustainability are separate questions. A developer may gain control over design without gaining reliable visibility, income, or long-term stability.

The literature also uses different methods and units of analysis. Historical research explains structural change across decades; conceptual articles clarify terms; qualitative studies describe developer experiences and labor; platform studies analyze technical and distribution infrastructures; and a game-specific case study reconstructs a creative process. These forms of evidence are complementary but not interchangeable. For example, an interview study can illuminate why developers make particular choices but cannot, by itself, measure an industry-wide effect. A case study can reconstruct a design process but cannot show that the same pattern is typical of all indie games.

A gap for the diploma project is therefore not a lack of claims that independent games can be innovative, but the need to connect claims about innovation to defined dimensions and traceable evidence. The project can address this by using a consistent comparative framework for selected games, distinguishing observable design characteristics from claims of influence, and recording the limitations of publicly available evidence. Case studies should be chosen purposefully, and conclusions should be proportional to the evidence collected.

The practical project, One More Day, can apply selected insights from this literature to the design of a small 2D narrative game. In particular, it can explore how narrative structure, dialogue, sound, and a consequential player choice are implemented under the constraints of a small independent project. It cannot demonstrate that independent development as a whole has changed the industry. Instead, it can serve as an applied design case that makes the research-to-practice connection explicit.

## Appendix A. Structured Analysis of the 15 Selected Publications

This matrix makes the evidence base behind the narrative review transparent by comparing all 15 selected publications across consistent fields. It is an analytical companion to the review, not a separate or systematic review.

- **Method / material** summarizes the method or source material described in the publication or its publisher/author record. Where a study is conceptual or the available record does not identify a standardized sample, the table avoids inferring one.
- **Scope / limitations** combines the study’s stated scope with critical-reading cautions for this diploma. These cautions are not necessarily limitations explicitly stated by the original authors.
- **Relevance** explains how the source informs this diploma’s research, comparison criteria, technical decisions, or project planning. It does not claim that a source directly validates *One More Day*.

The source-selection rationale and temporal scope are explained in Section 1. This matrix is a critical-reading aid based on the same 15-source set; it is not an exhaustive or systematically screened census of the field. Claims about present-day market conditions or platform behaviour should still be supported by current evidence in the final thesis.
